# Fragments

## musonius.rufus.22

One cannot live the present day well unless one has set it before oneself as one's last.

## musonius.rufus.23

Why do we reproach tyrants, when we ourselves are far worse than they? For we have the same impulses as they do, in less similar fortunes.

## musonius.rufus.24

Even if what is pleasing is to be measured by pleasure, nothing is pleasanter than temperance; and even if what is to be avoided is measured by toil, nothing is more laborious than intemperance.

## musonius.rufus.25

Musonius used to say that it is an excess of shamelessness to remember the weakness of the body when it comes to enduring toils, but to forget it when it comes to pleasures.

## musonius.rufus.26

The beginning of not shrinking from doing shameful things is not shrinking from saying them.

## musonius.rufus.27

If you choose above all to hold to what is advantageous, do not be vexed at circumstances, reflecting how many things in life have already befallen you not as you wished but as was to your advantage.

## musonius.rufus.28

Seize the chance to die well while it is possible, lest soon dying may be at hand for you, but dying well no longer.

## musonius.rufus.29

(Of the same.) A person who lives dutifully to the advantage of many should not die, unless in dying he is of advantage to more.

## musonius.rufus.30

You will be worthy of respect among all, if you begin first by respecting yourself.

## musonius.rufus.31

Those who, in dealing with their subjects, have practised saying about what they do not "It is my duty" but "I am permitted" do not last long.

## musonius.rufus.32

(Of the same.) Do not expect to command others to do their duty when they excuse you for doing what is not your duty.

## musonius.rufus.33

(Of the same.) Try to be seen by your subjects as awe-inspiring rather than fearsome; for dignity attends the one, and harshness the other.

## musonius.rufus.34

We pass the verdict of utter poverty on the treasures of Croesus and Cinyras, and we will believe that only one person is rich or wise: the one who is able to acquire freedom from need everywhere.

## musonius.rufus.35

Since dying has been spun alike for all, what is blessed is not to die slowly but to die in plain view with distinction.

## musonius.rufus.36

(Plutarch, addressed to Sulla.) Among the fine sayings of Musonius that we remember, Sulla, is this: that those who are to be saved must live always under treatment. For one must not, I think, after being treated as with hellebore, expel the reasoning together with the disease, but keep it and guard one's judgments, remaining in the soul. For the power of reasoning is not like drugs but like healthy foods, which produce together with health a good condition in those to whom it becomes habitual. Exhortations and admonitions given to passions that are at their height and swollen do their work slowly and with difficulty, and are no different from smelling salts, which rouse epileptics when they fall but do not rid them of the disease.

## musonius.rufus.37

The famous Rutilius, in Rome, approaching Musonius, said: "Musonius, Zeus the Savior, whom you imitate and emulate, does not borrow." Musonius smiled and said: "Nor does he lend." For Rutilius, being himself a lender, was reproaching Musonius as a borrower.

## musonius.rufus.38

(Rufus, in the work of Epictetus, On Friendship.) Of the things that exist, the god put some in our power and others not in our power. In our power is the most beautiful and serious thing, by which he himself too is happy: the use of impressions. When this is rightly done, it is freedom, a smooth flow of life, good cheer and steadiness; it is also justice, law, temperance and the whole of virtue. All other things he did not make in our power. We, then, must be of one mind with the god, and, dividing matters in this way, lay claim in every way to all that is in our power, and the things not in our power we should entrust to the universe, gladly yielding them, whether it asks for our children, our country, our body, or anything else.

## musonius.rufus.39

(Rufus, in Epictetus, On Friendship.) Who among us does not marvel at the story of Lycurgus the Spartan? A citizen had put out one of his eyes, and the people handed the young man over to him so that he might punish him as he wished. Lycurgus refrained from this; instead he educated him, showed him to be a good man, and led him into the theatre. When the Spartans were amazed, he said: "I received this man from you violent and brutal; I give him back to you decent and civic-minded."

## musonius.rufus.40

(Rufus, in Epictetus, On Friendship.) But above everything it is the work of nature to join and harmonize impulse with the impression of what is fitting and beneficial.

## musonius.rufus.41

(Of the same.) To think that we shall be easily despised by others, if we do not harm our first enemies by every means, is the thought of very ignoble and foolish people. For we say that the contemptible person is thought of as one who is unable to harm; but much more is he thought of as one who is unable to benefit.

## musonius.rufus.42

(Rufus, in Epictetus, On Friendship.) Such is the nature of the universe, such it was and will be, and it is not possible for things that come to be to be otherwise than as they are now. In this change not only human beings have shared, and other animals on earth, but also divine things; by Zeus, even the four elements themselves are turned up and down and changed: earth becomes water, water becomes air, and this changes again into aether, and the same way of change goes from above downward. If one tries to turn his mind to these things and persuade himself to accept necessities willingly, he will pass his life most moderately and harmoniously.

## musonius.rufus.43

Thrasea used to say: "Today I would rather be killed than tomorrow be exiled." What then did Rufus say to him? "If you choose it as the heavier, what foolishness in the choice; if as the lighter, who has given you the choice? Will you not practise being content with what has been given?"

## musonius.rufus.44

(Epictetus on his teacher.) Why are we still idle, lazy and slack, and why do we look for excuses for not working or staying awake, polishing the same argument over and over? "If then in these matters I err, I have not killed my father." "Slave, where was there a father here that you could kill? What then did you do? The one error possible in this place, this you committed." I said this very thing to Rufus too, when he reproached me because I could not find what had been omitted in a certain syllogism: "It is not as though I burned the Capitol." He said: "Slave, the omitted thing here is the Capitol." Or are these the only errors, to burn the Capitol and kill one's father, while to use one's impressions carelessly, in vain and just as it happens, and not to follow the argument, the proof or the fallacy, nor, in general, to see what follows and what does not follow from a thing in question and answer, is none of these an error?

## musonius.rufus.45

So too Rufus, testing me, used to say: "This and that will happen to you from your master." When I replied that these are human things, he said: "Why then should I appeal to him, when I can take them from you myself?" [The Greek here is corrupt; other reconstructions differ.] For in truth, whoever has something from himself finds it superfluous and silly to take it from another.

## musonius.rufus.46

It is not easy to urge the soft among the young toward philosophy; one cannot catch cheese with a hook. The well-endowed, even if you turn them away, cling to the argument all the more. For this reason Rufus too used to discourage most people, using this as a test of the gifted and the ungifted. He used to say: "As a stone, even if you throw it up, will be carried down toward its own construction, so also the well-endowed person: the more one pushes him back, the more he inclines toward what he is naturally suited for."

## musonius.rufus.47

Someone said to Rufus, when Galba had been slain: "Is the world now governed by providence?" And he replied: "Did I ever, even as a side remark, prove from Galba that the world is governed by providence?"

## musonius.rufus.48

Rufus used to say: "If you have leisure to praise me, I am saying nothing worthwhile." Accordingly he used to speak in such a way that each of us, sitting there, supposed that someone had slandered him to Rufus: so closely did he touch on what was going on, so he set before each man's eyes his own evils.

## musonius.rufus.49

(Aulus Gellius, Attic Nights V.1; Latin.) We have heard that the philosopher Musonius used to say: "When a philosopher exhorts, warns, advises, rebukes, or discusses any other point of doctrine, then if the listeners, in response, burst out with commonplace praise from a sound, relaxed heart, if they shout, if they gesticulate, if they are moved by the charms of his voice, the measure of his words, or certain rhythms of speech, then you should know that both the speaker and the listeners are wasting their time, and that the philosopher is not speaking but a flute-player is playing. The mind of the person who is listening to a philosopher, while what is said is useful and healthy and brings a remedy for errors and vices, has no leisure for praising at length and profusely. Whoever he is who is listening, unless he is utterly lost, must shudder in the middle of the philosopher's speech, and be silent with shame, and feel remorse, and rejoice, and marvel, and show such varied expressions and such different feelings, according as the philosopher's treatment has affected his conscience and the parts of his soul, whether sound or sick."

He used to say, besides, that great praise does not lack admiration, but admiration, which is greatest, produces not words but silence. "For this reason," he said, "the wisest of poets, telling most illustriously of Ulysses' labours to his hearers, where the end of the speaking is reached, does not make them exult or clap or shout, but says that all were silent, as though stunned and held fast by a charm in their ears, the sound still lingering in them to their depths:

'So he spoke; and they all became hushed in silence, spellbound throughout the shadowy halls.'" (Odyssey 13.1–2)

## musonius.rufus.50

(Aulus Gellius, Attic Nights IX.2; Latin.) Herodes said: "Musonius, when a certain beggar of that kind, displaying himself as a philosopher, was ordered to be given a thousand sesterces, and when most people said he was a scoundrel, a bad and malicious man, deserving nothing good, is said to have remarked with a slight smile: 'Then he is worthy of money.'"

## musonius.rufus.51

(Aulus Gellius, Attic Nights XVI.1; Latin.) When we were still youths in the schools, we heard this Greek maxim, which I have appended, said by the philosopher Musonius, and because it is truly and brilliantly spoken, bound in short and rounded words, we gladly remembered it: "If you accomplish something noble with toil, the toil goes away, the noble remains; if you do something shameful with pleasure, the pleasure goes away, the shame remains." Later we read this same thought in Cato's speech, which he delivered at Numantia before the cavalry. Though it is expressed in somewhat looser and longer words than the Greek, since it is earlier in time and older it ought to seem more venerable. These are the words from the speech: "Think in your minds: if you do something right through labour, that labour will quickly leave you, the good deed will not leave you as long as you live; but if you do something base through pleasure, the pleasure will quickly pass, the base deed will remain with you forever."

## musonius.rufus.52

(Latin.) "To relax the mind," said Musonius, "is as it were to lose it."

## musonius.rufus.53

(Aelius Aristides.) Someone, urging me to take courage, recounted a saying of Musonius. "That man," he said, "wanting to raise up someone who was ailing and had given up, said, taking hold of him: 'Why are you waiting? Where are you looking? Is it until the god himself stands beside you and sends forth a voice? Cut out the dead part of your soul, and you will know the god.'" Such things, he said, Musonius said.
