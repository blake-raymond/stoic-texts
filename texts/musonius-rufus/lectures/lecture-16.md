# XVI. Whether one must obey one's parents in everything

A young man whose father did not want him to study philosophy asked Musonius something like this: "Is it right, Musonius, to obey one's parents in everything, or are there things in which one should disobey?" Musonius said: "To obey his mother or father seems to me a noble thing in every case, and I praise it. But what does obeying consist in? Let us look first at what disobeying is and who the disobedient person is, and then we shall see what obeying is.

"Come now, suppose a sick father, who is not a doctor and has no experience of the healthy or the sick, prescribes something as beneficial that would in fact be harmful and unprofitable, and fails to realize that it is of this sort. Would the one who does not do what was ordered be disobedient? It does not seem so. And what if a father, being sick, asks for wine or food at the wrong time, which if he took it would make his illness worse, and the son, knowing this, does not give it? Is he disobedient to his father? One cannot say so. And far less, I think, would one say a man is disobedient who, when his father, being greedy for gain, orders him to steal or to defraud someone of a deposit, does not carry out the command. Or do you think there are no fathers who give such orders to their own children? I know some who were so wicked that they sold their son in the prime of his youth. If, then, the young man who was sold, when sent by his father to his shame, resisted and did not go, would we say that he was disobedient, or temperate? Is this even worth asking? For disobeying and being disobedient is a matter of reproach and censure; but not doing what one must not do is no reproach, but praise.

"So whether it is a father's order or a ruler's or, by Zeus, a master's, if a person does not comply when he orders evil, unjust or shameful things, he is not disobedient at all, just as he neither wrongs nor errs. The only disobedient person is the one who neglects and pays no attention to the orders of those who give them well, nobly and beneficially. That is what the disobedient man is like. The obedient man is the opposite to this: one who, listening to the person advising him what is proper, follows willingly. Hence a person also obeys his parents when they exhort him to good things and he does them willingly. I, however, say that even a person who does what he should and what benefits him, though his parents do not exhort him to it, obeys his parents. That I am right, consider this: the person who does what his father wants and follows his father's wishes obeys his father; and the person who does what he should and what is best follows his father's wishes. How? Because all parents wish their children well, I take it, and, wishing them well, want what is right and beneficial to be done by them. So whoever does what is fitting and beneficial does what his parents want. Thus he obeys his parents in doing this, even if the parents do not order him in words to do it. This alone should be considered, by anyone who wants to obey his parents in each thing he does: whether what he is about to do is noble and beneficial, and nothing else, since if such is the case the one who does it straightway obeys his parents.

"So do not you, young man, be afraid that you will be disobedient to your father, whether he orders you to do what you should not and you abstain from doing it, or he forbids what you should do and you do it. And let your father's error not be a pretext for you, if he orders something shameful to be done or forbids something noble. No necessity lies on you to comply with orders that are badly given; and I think your father himself does not fail to know this. Would your father not put up with it in music, if he, knowing no music, ordered you to play the lyre in an unmusical way? Or, not knowing letters, ordered you to write and read not as you were taught but differently? And if a man who knew nothing of steering, not being a pilot, ordered you to move the rudder in a way that was not appropriate, you would pay no attention to him. What then? So it stands. If your father prevents you from philosophizing, not knowing or having heard what philosophy is, being himself ignorant of it, should one pay attention to him, or rather teach him that he is not advising you well? So it seems to me.

"Perhaps a person using argument alone might persuade his own father to think about philosophy as he should, at least if the father is not completely harsh by nature. If he is not persuaded by the argument and does not follow, the son's deeds at least will win him over entirely, if the son truly philosophizes. For he will be the most eager of philosophers to serve his father with every kind of service, the most orderly and gentle, the least quarrelsome or self-loving in their association, neither rash nor agitated nor irritable. He will also be master of his tongue, his belly and his sexual desires, enduring in face of terrors and toils; he will understand as fully as possible what is noble, without going beyond what appears noble. For this reason he will yield to his father in all pleasant things willingly, and will take on the laborious ones before him. Who would not pray to the gods to have such a son? And who, having him, would not be content, since through him he would be an object of emulation and the most blessed father in the eyes of all sensible people?

"If then, young man, being such as you would be if you truly philosophize, your father does not let you or persuade you to do and share in these things, consider this: your father forbids you to philosophize, but the common father of all human beings and gods, Zeus, bids and urges you. For his command and law is that a human being be just, good, beneficent, temperate, high-minded, superior to toil, superior to pleasure, and clean of all envy and plotting. In short, the law of Zeus bids the human being be good. And being good is the same as being a philosopher. If, then, you are going to obey your father, you will follow a human; if you philosophize, Zeus; clearly you should rather philosophize than not.

"But, by Zeus, your father will confine you and lock you up so that you cannot philosophize. This he may perhaps do, but he will not prevent you from philosophizing if you do not wish to be prevented. For we philosophize not by hand or foot or the rest of the body, but by the soul, and by a small part of it, which we call the mind. This the god set in the most fortified place, so that it is invisible and untouchable, and free of all necessity and self-governing. Especially if it happens to be good, your father will not be able to prevent you from using your mind, or from thinking what you should, or from pleasing what is noble and not pleasing what is shameful, or from choosing some things and avoiding others. By doing these you will be a philosopher at once, and you will not need to wear a rough cloak, or go around without a tunic, or wear long hair, or step outside the common way of the many. These things, too, suit philosophers; but philosophizing does not consist in them; it consists in thinking and understanding what one should."
