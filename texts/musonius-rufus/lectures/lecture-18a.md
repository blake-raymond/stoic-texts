# XVIII A. On food

He often spoke about food, and very intensively, as a matter that is neither small nor concerned with small things; for he thought that the beginning and foundation of temperance is self-control in food and drink. Once, setting aside the other arguments, which he explained each time, he said something like this. Just as one should prefer cheap food to costly, and food that is easy to obtain to food that is hard to obtain, so too one should prefer what is akin to human beings over what is not. Akin to us is what comes from the growth of the earth: whatever is grain, and whatever else can nourish a human being without harm; and what comes from animals that are not killed but are otherwise put to use. Of these foods the most suitable are those that can be used just as they are, without fire, since they are also the most ready to hand: such as fruits, some vegetables, milk, cheese and honeycombs. Those that need fire, grain foods or vegetable foods, are also not unsuitable; all of these are akin to human beings. A meat diet he declared more bestial and more suited to wild animals. He said that it is also heavier and an impediment to thinking and understanding, for the exhalation from it, being muddier, clouds the soul; for this reason those who use it more appear slower in mind.

A human being, he said, as the creature on earth nearest in kinship to the gods, should also be nourished in the way most like the gods. For the gods the vapours that rise from earth and water suffice; and we, he said, should offer ourselves a nourishment as similar to this as possible: the lightest and the cleanest. In this way our soul would also be pure and dry, as it would be best and wisest, as Heraclitus seems to say: "A dry beam of light is the wisest and best soul." But now, he said, we are fed far worse than the irrational animals. For these, even if they are driven by desire toward food as though by a whip, are exempt from being clever about foods and from cooking; they are content with what is at hand and satisfied merely with what is near, and nothing further. We, however, invent manifold arts and contrivances, so as to flatter the sweetness of food and the pleasure of consumption all the more. We have advanced so far in dainty living and relish-eating that, as treatises on music and medicine have been composed, so have some on cookery, which greatly increase the pleasure in the throat but ruin health. One can see that the bodies of those who are fastidious about their food are in much worse condition; some of them are like pregnant women with cravings. For these men too, like those women, loathe the most familiar foods and have ruined stomachs. Hence, just as a useless piece of iron needs constant sharpening, so their stomachs, in eating, want to be constantly sharpened by unmixed wine or vinegar or something sour. But the Spartan was not like that. Seeing a man who, with a fat and costly bird set before him, refused through luxury to eat it and said he could not, he said: "But I can eat even vulture and lammergeier." And Zeno of Citium did not think that even the sick should be offered more luxurious food. When the doctor who was treating him told him to eat a young pigeon, he would not put up with it, and said: "Treat me like Manes." He thought, I suppose, that nothing softer should be provided in his treatment than for a slave who is sick; for if they can be treated without being given costlier food, we can be too. The good man must in no way be softer than any slave. Hence Zeno reasonably thought one should be on guard against luxury in food and not give way even a little, since once one has given way, it goes on to the furthest extent, because pleasure grows greatly in drink and food. These, then, were the rather novel things that he said then about food, as it seemed to us, compared with what he used to say each time.
