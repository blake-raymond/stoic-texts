# XX. On furnishings

Extravagance about furnishings seems to be of a piece with and akin to that about houses, and the furnishings in the house: couches, tables, coverings, drinking cups and anything of the kind, going entirely beyond what use requires and reaching further than what is necessary. There are couches of ivory and silver, or, by Zeus, gold; tables of similar material; coverings of purple and others hard to come by; cups made of gold and silver, and of stone set with silver and gold. And all this is pursued, although a three-legged pallet provides us a bed no worse than a silver or ivory couch, and a rough blanket is quite sufficient to spread over it, so that we do not need purple or scarlet. We can eat harmlessly from a wooden table, so that we have no need of silver; and drink, by Zeus, from earthenware cups, which quench thirst just as the gold ones do, and the wine poured in is not spoiled, and gives off a fragrance as pleasant as from gold and silver.

In general, the virtue and vice of furnishings could rightly be judged by three things: acquisition, use, and keeping. What is hard to acquire, or unsuitable to use, or not easy to keep is worse; what we acquire without difficulty and use easily we praise, and we keep it readily, and that is better. Hence earthenware and iron and the like are much better than silver and gold, since the acquisition of these is easier, as they are cheaper, and their use is more varied, since we can readily put them on the fire, which we cannot do with the other; and their keeping is less troublesome, as cheap things are less plotted against by the greedy. Part of keeping is also cleaning, and for costly things this is greater. Just as a horse purchased for little, but giving much use, is preferable to one dearly bought but giving little use, so also furnishings that are cheap and have many uses are better than their opposites.

Why then do people pursue the rare and costly in preference to the common and cheap? Because they do not know what is truly noble and good, and instead of what truly exists, they pursue among the foolish what only seems so; just as madmen often take white for black. Folly is closely akin to madness. Among lawgivers, the best, and one of the first we would find, is Lycurgus, who banished luxury from Sparta and introduced thrift and a lack of what is superfluous in living, preferring it for the sake of courage, driving out luxury as a ruin and thinking that love of toil deserves to be emulated as a salvation. Proofs of this are the endurance trials of the youths there, trained to bear hunger, thirst, cold and heat, and also blows and other hardships, with solemn customs. The ancient Spartans, raised in this way, were the best of the Greeks and were reputed so, and made their poverty more envied than the king's wealth.

As for me, I would sooner accept being sick than living in luxury. Sickness harms only the body, but luxury corrupts both, soul and body, producing weakness and powerlessness in the body, and intemperance and unmanliness in the soul. Luxury also begets injustice, because it begets greed. It is not possible for a luxurious person not to be extravagant; nor, being extravagant, to want to spend little; but wishing to spend much and not trying to provide much, nor, trying to provide much, not to be grasping and unjust; no one could provide much from just sources. Moreover, the luxurious man must be unjust in another way: if he had to suffer hardship for friends or kin, he would not endure it, for luxury will not allow it. And sometimes the just man must even toil for the gods, if he wants to be right toward them, since he will perform sacrifices or initiations or some other service for the gods, while the luxurious person will fall short here as well. So he would be unjust in every way, toward his city, friends and gods, not doing what he should. Since luxury is a cause of injustice, it must be avoided by every means.
