# IV. Whether daughters should be educated like sons

When the question came up whether sons and daughters should receive the same education, he said: Horse trainers and hunters train horses and dogs with no difference between males and females; female dogs are taught to hunt just as males are, and if one wants mares to do a horse's work well, one sees them given the same instruction as stallions. But for human beings, is it then necessary that males have something special in their education and upbringing that females do not? As if the same virtues did not have to be present in man and woman alike, or as if it were possible to reach the same virtues not through the same training but through different ones!

That the virtues of a man are not one thing and a woman's another is easy to see. For example, a man must be prudent, and a woman as well; otherwise what use would a senseless man or woman be? Then each must live justly, neither less than the other. A man who is unjust would not be a good citizen, and a woman who is unjust would not manage her household well; being unjust, she would wrong her husband, as they say Eriphyle did. Temperance too is good in a woman, and equally good in a man; at any rate the laws punish adultery on equal terms in the one who commits it and the one who suffers it. Gluttony, drunkenness and other similar evils are forms of intemperance and bring great shame on those caught in them; they show that temperance is most necessary for every human being, female and male alike. For only through temperance do we escape licentiousness; in no other way.

Courage, one might say, perhaps belongs only to men. But this is not so either. A woman must be brave and free of cowardice, at least in the highest degree, so as to give way neither to toil nor to fear. If she cannot, how will she still be temperate, if someone, either by frightening her or by laying toil on her, can force her to endure something shameful? Women must also be able to defend themselves, unless, by Zeus, they want to look worse than hens and other female birds, who fight for their young against animals much larger than themselves. How, then, would women not need courage? That women share in strength of arms as well, the race of the Amazons showed, who subdued many peoples by force of arms. So if other women lack something in this respect, it is lack of practice rather than a failure of nature... the virtues of man and woman [are the same], and it follows of necessity that the same nurture and education suit both.

For to every animal and plant one must apply the care that rightly instils in it the excellence proper to it. If it were necessary that man and woman alike be able to play the flute, and if each of them needed it for living, we would teach both the flute equally; and if each had to play the cithara... [gap]. But if both must become good, with the virtue that suits a human being, and be equally able to be prudent and to share in temperance, courage and justice, no less the one than the other, shall we not train each alike, nor teach equally the art through which a person becomes good? We should do so, and no other way.

"What, then?" someone may say. "Do you think men should learn spinning and weaving as women do, and women take part in gymnastic training like men?" This I would no longer claim. But I say that since in the human race the nature of males is the stronger and that of females the weaker, the tasks most suited to each should be assigned; the heavier to the stronger, the lighter to the weaker. Therefore spinning and housekeeping suit women more than men, while gymnastics and outdoor work suit men more than women. Sometimes, though, some men might reasonably do some of the lighter work called women's, and women in turn some of the harder work that is thought to suit men more, when the condition of the body, need, or circumstance so suggests. For all human tasks are perhaps held in common, are common to men and women, and nothing is reserved by necessity for one or the other. Some are better suited to this nature and some to that; this is why some are called men's work and others women's. But whatever has reference to virtue, one could rightly say suits both alike by nature, if we agree that virtues suit no one more than others.

So it is reasonable, I think, that all that pertains to virtue be learned similarly by female and male. Starting from infancy we should teach right away that this is good and that is bad, the same for both; this is beneficial and that harmful; this should be done and that should not. From this prudence comes to learners, girls and boys alike, with no difference between them. Then a sense of shame toward everything shameful must be implanted in them. Once this has been done, both man and woman will necessarily be temperate. And the person who is rightly educated, whoever he or she is, male or female, must be accustomed to endure toil, accustomed not to fear death, accustomed not to be humbled by any misfortune; through all this one becomes brave. It was shown a little earlier that women too must have a share in courage. Moreover, to avoid greed, honour equality, and be willing to do good and unwilling to do harm to people, being a human being oneself, is a most beautiful lesson and makes those who learn it just. What more reason is there that a man should have learned these things? If, by Zeus, women are to be just, they too must have learned these most important and greatest lessons.

If, somewhere, the man knows some small thing and the woman does not, or conversely she knows and he does not, as a matter of some craft, that does not yet make the education of the two different. Only let neither learn different things about any of the greatest matters, but the same. If someone asks me what branch of knowledge presides over this education, I will tell him that without philosophy, just as no man, so no woman either could be rightly educated. I do not mean that women need any exceptional fluency or cleverness in speech, if they are to philosophize as women; I do not much praise that even in men. What I mean is that women must acquire goodness of character and nobility of conduct, since philosophy is the pursuit of nobility, and nothing else.
