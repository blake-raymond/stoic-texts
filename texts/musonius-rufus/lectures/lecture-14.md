# XIV. Whether marriage is an obstacle to philosophy

When someone else said that marriage and life with a woman seemed to him to be an obstacle to philosophy, Musonius said: "It was not an obstacle to Pythagoras, nor to Socrates, nor to Crates, each of whom lived with a woman; and one could not name better philosophers than those. Crates was completely without house, equipment and possessions, and yet he married. He then spent his days and nights with his wife, without even sandals, in the public stoas of Athens. And we, who start out from a house, and some of whom have servants to wait on us, still dare to say that marriage is an obstacle to philosophy?

"Moreover, the philosopher is surely a teacher and leader of all human beings in what is natural to a human being; and marrying seems to be natural if anything is. Why else did the maker of human beings first divide our kind in two, then make for it two sets of private parts, one female and one male, and then implant a strong desire in each for the company and union with the other, and fill both with a strong longing for each other, the male for the female and the female for the male? Is it not clear that he wanted them to be together and live together, and to devise together the means of life, and to produce children and rear them together, so that our race might be everlasting?

"What then? Tell me: is it fitting for each person to do only what concerns himself, or also what concerns his neighbour: how households may be established in the city, how the city may not be left empty, and how the common good may be well served? If you say that one should consider only oneself, you declare the human being no different from a wolf or any other of the wildest animals, which are by nature made to live by force and greed, sparing nothing from which they will get some pleasure; creatures with no share in society, in cooperation, or in anything just. But if you agree that the human being is most like the bee, which cannot live alone (for it perishes when alone), and joins with those of its kind for a single common work, and works with them and cooperates with its neighbours; if this is so, and if, further, vice is taken to be injustice, savagery, and taking poor care of one's neighbour's affairs, and virtue to be love of humanity, kindness, justice, and doing good and caring for one's neighbour, then each person must take thought for his city and furnish the city with households. The beginning of building a household is marriage. So whoever removes marriage from among humans removes household, removes city, removes the whole human race. For we would not continue to exist without procreation, and there would be no procreation without marriage, at least a just and lawful one.

"That a household or city is made up not of women alone, nor of men alone, but of the partnership of each with the other, is clear; and no other partnership of men and women could one find that is either more necessary or more affectionate. What comrade is so well disposed to comrade as a wife is devoted to her husband? What brother to brother? What son to his parents? Who, when absent, is so longed for as a husband by a wife and a wife by a husband? Whose presence would more lighten grief, or increase joy, or set right a misfortune? For whom are all things thought to be common, bodies, souls, and property, except for man and woman? That is why all people consider the love of husband and wife the most venerable of all, and why no mother or father with sense would be dearer to his own child than the partner in marriage. And the story shows how much the love between husband and wife surpasses that of parents for children: Admetus, having received this gift from the gods, that if he could provide someone to die in his place he would live twice the time fated for him, did not find his parents willing to die before him, although they were old; but his wife Alcestis, who was young, readily accepted death in her husband's place.

"That marriage is great and worthy of serious regard is shown also by this: the gods oversee it, great gods, as people believe. First Hera, and for this reason we call her Zygia, goddess of the yoke; then Eros, then Aphrodite; for we suppose that all these have this work, to bring man and woman together for procreation. Where would Eros be summoned more justly than at the lawful union of a man and a woman? Where Hera? Where Aphrodite? When could one pray more opportunely to these gods than when going to marriage? What would we call a work of Aphrodite more fittingly than the wife's union with her husband? Why, then, would anyone say that the gods who oversee and manage marriage and procreation so greatly are not concerned with human beings in these matters? Why would these things befit a human being but not befit the philosopher? Is it that the philosopher should be worse than the others? On the contrary, he should be better, more just, and more noble. Or is the man who does not care for his own city not worse and more unjust than the one who does, and the man who looks only to himself than the one who looks to the common good? Or is a man who chooses a solitary life more devoted to his city, humane and sociable than the man who runs a household, makes children, and makes his city grow, which is what the married man does? That it is fitting for the philosopher to take care of marriage and procreation is clear. If this is fitting, how could that argument you were just now putting forward, that marriage is an obstacle to philosophy, be right? For philosophizing is nothing other than seeking by argument what is proper and fitting, and doing it in deed."

So much, then, is what he said on that occasion.
