# I. That one should not use many proofs for a single matter

When the talk once turned to the proofs that young people should hear from philosophers in order to grasp what they are learning, Musonius said that one should not look for proofs on every single point, but for proofs that are useful and clear. A doctor, he said, is not praised for bringing many drugs to the sick, but for curing them with a few; nor is a philosopher worth praising who teaches his hearers through many proofs, but one who benefits them through a few, bringing them to what he intends. The hearer, too, the more intelligent he is, the fewer proofs he will need, and the sooner he will agree with the main point of the argument, provided that point is sound. But anyone who needs proof everywhere, even where things are plain, or who wants to be shown through many proofs what can be shown through few, is quite absurd and slow to learn.

It is reasonable that the gods need no proof of anything, since nothing is unclear or hidden to them; and it is only for such unclear things that proofs are needed. Human beings, however, cannot know what is not evident except through what is evident and already known, so they must seek and discover it, and that is the very work of proof. For example, that pleasure is not a good is not known in itself, since pleasure itself calls us to it as if it were a good. But if someone takes a known premise, that everything good is choiceworthy, and adds a further known premise, that some pleasures are not choiceworthy, we prove that pleasure is not a good, the unknown through what is known. Again, that toil is not an evil is not convincing in itself; the opposite seems more convincing, that toil is an evil. But once an evident premise is posited, that every evil is to be avoided, and a still more evident one is added, that many kinds of toil are not things to be avoided, the conclusion is reached that toil is not an evil. Since proof is of this kind, and since human beings differ (some are sharper, some duller, some better and some worse in their upbringing and habits, some worse in their character or nature), the latter would need more proofs and a greater effort, in order to accept these doctrines and be stamped by them. This is just as bodily ailments, I think, need great care when the body is to be restored to health. But those young people who are more gifted and have had some training can accept what is said more easily, more quickly, and through few proofs, and follow it rightly.

That this is so, we could easily see if we pictured a boy or a young man who has been raised in complete luxury, with a body gone soft and a soul dissolved into softness by habits that were never checked, and who in addition is of a poor and slow-learning nature. Picture another, raised in somewhat Spartan fashion, unused to luxury and practised in endurance, and obedient to what is said. Then picture these two young men hearing a philosopher speak about death, about toil, saying that they are not evils; again about poverty, saying that it is not bad; and about life, pleasure and wealth, saying that they are not goods. Would both be persuaded in the same way, and would each be moved similarly by what is said? It is impossible to say so. The first, the duller one, would hardly and slowly give way, as if pried with levers by ten thousand arguments. The other would quickly and readily accept what is said as akin and fitting to him, needing neither many proofs nor greater effort. Was he not just like that Spartan boy who asked the philosopher Cleanthes whether toil is a good? So well, it seems, was that boy by nature suited and trained toward virtue that he thought toil nearer to the nature of the good than to that of evil. He asked Cleanthes his question as though it were agreed that toil is not an evil, wondering whether it might be good. Hence Cleanthes, delighted with the boy, said: "Of good blood you are, dear child, to speak as you do" (Homer, Odyssey 4.611). How, then, would such a person not be easily persuaded neither to fear poverty or death or any other thing that seems fearful, nor to pursue wealth, life or pleasure?

To return to the beginning of the discussion: I say that the teacher of philosophy should not seek a multitude of arguments or proofs to run through with his students, but should speak at the right moment on each matter and impress the hearer's mind, [and offer arguments that are] persuasive and not easily overturned. Above all he should do so by offering himself as an example, speaking about the most useful matters and doing what agrees with what he says; the hearers should be handled in this way. The student, for his part, must be intent on what is said, and take care that he does not unknowingly accept something false; and, by Zeus, he should not seek many proofs of the truths, but listen to clear ones; and what he is persuaded of among the things advised, which is both his own and true, he should follow in his life. For only in this way will anyone be helped by philosophy: if, among the arguments he receives, none is unsound, and he provides actions that are in harmony with them.
