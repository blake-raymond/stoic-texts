# VIII. That kings too should philosophize

When one of the kings from Syria (for there were at that time still kings in Syria, subject to the Romans) came to see him, Musonius said many things to the man, among them the following.

"Do not think," he said, "that philosophy befits anyone more than you, or that anyone has more reason to philosophize than that you happen to be a king. For a king must surely be able to save and benefit people; and one who will save and benefit them must know what is good for a human being and what bad, what is useful and what harmful, what is advantageous and what is disadvantageous, since those who run into evils perish, those who obtain goods are saved, those thought worthy of useful and advantageous things are benefited, and those thrown into disadvantageous and harmful things are hurt. To discern good and bad, advantage and disadvantage, benefit and harm, is the work of no one else than the philosopher, who spends all his time busied with just this, so as to be ignorant of none of these things, and who has made it his art to know what brings human beings happiness or misery. So the king, it appears, must philosophize.

"Moreover, it is the king's duty, or rather his necessity, to judge his subjects justly, neither having more himself nor depriving anyone contrary to his deserts, but giving honour and punishment to those who deserve them. How could anyone do this unless he is just? And how could anyone be just who does not know justice, what sort of thing it is? So the king must philosophize in this respect too, since he would not otherwise appear to know justice and what is just unless he philosophized. For neither would the person who has learned know what is just any better than the one who has not, nor are all who have not philosophized ignorant in these matters. They quarrel and fight among themselves about what is just, some asserting that this is more just and others that that is. And yet about the things people know they do not differ: not about white or black, or hot or cold, or soft or hard; about these, everyone thinks and speaks alike. So they would agree about just things in the same way, if they knew what they were; but where they do not agree they appear to be ignorant. And you, I think, are not outside this ignorance. So you must devote yourself to the knowledge of it more than anyone else, and it is so much more shameful for a king than for a private person to be ignorant about justice.

"Furthermore, the king must himself be temperate, and the subjects must be temperate, so that the ruler rules temperately and the ruled are ruled in good order and neither are luxurious; luxury is ruinous to ruler and private person alike. How could anyone be temperate without having practised mastery over his desires, or, being intemperate himself, make others temperate? What knowledge leads to temperance, other than philosophy, it is impossible to say. Philosophy teaches us to be above pleasure, teaches us to be above greed, teaches us to love simplicity and to flee extravagance. It accustoms us to feel shame and to control the tongue, and it produces order, decorum, good conduct, and in general what is fitting in movement and posture. These qualities make a person worthy of reverence and temperate. And a king who has them would be especially godlike and worthy of respect.

"Being without fear, unshaken and bold is the work of courage. How could a human being possess these in any other way than by gaining a strong conviction about death and toil, that they are not evils? For these are what frighten and make people shrink: death and toil, when they are persuaded that these are evils. That these are not evils, only philosophy teaches. So if kings must acquire courage, they must take care to philosophize, since they could not become courageous in any other way.

"Above all, it is kingly, if anything is, to be unconquered in argument and able to prevail over those who dispute with him, as one prevails with weapons over those who fight. If kings are weak in this, they are often necessarily led around and forced to accept falsehoods as true, which is the work of folly and the extreme of ignorance. I do not know that philosophy gives anything to those who pursue it more naturally than this: to prevail over one's neighbours in argument, to distinguish the false from the true, and to refute some claims and confirm others. Take orators, for example. When they come together with philosophers, to give and take argument, you can see them colliding, at a loss, forced to say things contrary to themselves. If orators, whose business is practising speech, are shown to be weaker in argument than philosophers, what are we to think of other people? So if a king has any desire to prevail in argument, he should philosophize, so that he need neither fear nor worry that anyone will get the better of him; the king must be fearless, confident and unconquered everywhere.

"In general, the good king must be, in word and deed, faultless and perfect, if he must, as the ancients thought, be a living law: devising good order and concord, preventing lawlessness and faction, and zealously imitating Zeus, being, as Zeus is, father of those he rules. How could anyone be like that unless he is superior by nature, has had the best education, and has all the virtues that suit a human being? If there is some other knowledge that leads a human being toward virtue and teaches him to practise and pursue noble things, it should be compared and examined, to see whether that or philosophy is better, and sufficient to show a king to be better. A king who wants to be good would then reasonably use the better one. But if no other art promises or teaches virtue, and the others deal with the human body and what is useful to it, or, when they touch the soul, consider anything rather than how it will become temperate, whereas philosophy alone looks at this and devises how a human being may escape vice and acquire virtue, then what else could be more necessary for a king who wants to be good than to philosophize? Or rather, how or in what way could anyone reign or live well unless he philosophized? I think the good king is at once and by necessity a philosopher, and the philosopher at once also kingly.

"Let us look first at the former. Is there any way a king can be good without being a good man? It is impossible to say so. And would a man who is good not also be a philosopher? By Zeus, if philosophy is the practice of nobility. So the king who is good is necessarily found to be a philosopher as well. That the philosopher is kingly you can learn like this. It is the mark of kingly skill to be able to take good care of the customs of peoples and cities, and to be worthy to rule human beings. Who would be more capable of leading a city, or more worthy to rule over human beings, than the philosopher? As he ought to be (if he is truly a philosopher), he is prudent, temperate and high-minded; a judge of what is just and fitting; effective in carrying out what he has thought out; steadfast in toils; and in addition fearless, unshaken, and able to stand firm against things that seem terrible; and moreover a kindly benefactor, humane. Who could be found more suitable or more capable of ruling than such a man? No one. And if he does not have many who obey and follow him, he is not for this reason deprived of being kingly. For it is enough to rule friends who are present, or wife and children, or even, by Zeus, only himself. A doctor is no less a doctor when treating few than when treating many, as long as he has medical experience; a musician is no less a musician when teaching few than when teaching many, if he has music; and a horseman is no less one when he uses one or two horses than when he uses many, if he knows horsemanship. In the same way a king who has many subjects, and one who has one or two who obey him, need only possess the experience of kingship, and he would be a king. For this reason it seems to me that Socrates too called philosophy the political and kingly science, because whoever takes it up is immediately a political man."

When Musonius had spoken in this way, the king was pleased with his words and was grateful, and agreed with what had been said to him. "In return for these things," he said, "ask of me whatever you wish; I will not refuse you anything." Musonius replied: "I ask you to follow these words which you praise. This would please me most, and you yourself would be benefited, and in no other way."
