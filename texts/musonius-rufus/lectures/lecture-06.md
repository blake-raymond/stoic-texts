# VI. On training

He always urged those who spent time with him to training, using arguments something like these. "Virtue," he said, "is not only theoretical knowledge but also practical, like medicine and music. So just as the doctor and the musician must not only have mastered the theory of his own art, but also be trained to act according to it, so the man who is to be good must not only learn thoroughly the lessons that lead to virtue, but also train himself according to them, with zeal and hard work.

"For how could anyone straightaway become temperate, if he only knew that one must not be defeated by pleasures, but were untrained in resisting them? How could anyone become just, having learned that one should love equality, but having had no practice in fleeing greed? How could we acquire courage, having understood that what seems terrible to the many is not truly fearful, but having had no practice in being unafraid of it? How could we become prudent, having discerned which things are truly good and which are bad, but being unexercised in despising what only seems good? Therefore each lesson that belongs to virtue must be followed by training, if our learning is to be of any use to us. And the person who claims to philosophize should train himself all the more than the one who pursues medicine or some similar art, as the thing he aims at is greater and harder to accomplish"


"In the other arts, those who aim at them have not had their souls corrupted beforehand, nor learned things opposed to what they are about to learn. But those who try to philosophize have been corrupted, are full of much that is evil, and are deeply entangled in vice; they pursue virtue, so that it too requires more training. How, then, and in what manner should they train? Since a human being is neither only a soul nor only a body, but something composed of these two, the person who trains must care for both: more for the better part, the soul, as it is the more worthy; and also for the other, if he is not to lack any part of the human being. For the body must also be well prepared for the works that the body of a philosopher has to do, since the virtues often use it as a necessary instrument for the actions of life.

"Some training belongs to the soul alone, and some is common to the soul and the body. Training common to both will occur when we accustom ourselves to cold, heat, thirst, hunger, meagre food, a hard bed, abstaining from pleasant things, and enduring painful ones. By these and similar practices the body is strengthened, becomes hardy and sturdy and useful for every task, and the soul is strengthened by being exercised: through enduring hardship toward courage, and through abstaining from pleasures toward temperance. Training of the soul in its own right consists first in making ready the proofs about things that seem good but are not, and about things that seem bad but are not; in recognizing true goods and distinguishing them from what is not truly good; and then in practising neither to flee any of the seeming evils nor to pursue any of the seeming goods, to shun by every device what is truly bad, and to pursue by every means what is truly good.

"I have now said in outline almost what each kind of training is. I shall try to say how each part should be done, not separating the exercises common to soul and body from those of the soul alone, but mixing the parts of each. Since we have been taught and have understood (those of us who have taken part in a philosopher's discourse) that neither toil nor death nor poverty is in any way an evil, nor anything else that is free of vice, and again that wealth, life, pleasure or anything else that lacks a share in virtue is not a good; and yet since, because of the corruption in us from childhood and our habit of evil, we think of toil as approaching us as an evil, and of pleasure as arriving as a good; we shudder at death as the last of misfortunes, and welcome life as the greatest of goods; when we give away money we grieve as though harmed, and when we receive it we rejoice as though benefited; and likewise in many other matters we do not act in accordance with the right convictions but follow our bad habits. Since, I say, this is so, the person who trains must seek to master himself in these: not to be delighted by pleasure, not to flinch from toil, not to cling to life, not to fear death, and, in matters of money, not to value receiving above giving."
