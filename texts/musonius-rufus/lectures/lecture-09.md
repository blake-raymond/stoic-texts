# IX. That exile is not an evil

When someone lamented that he was in exile, Musonius comforted him in this way.

"How could anyone not senseless be burdened by exile? Exile does not keep us from water, earth, or air, or the sun and the other stars, and does not shut us off from human company either, since all these things are shared everywhere. If we are cut off from some part of the earth and the company of some people, what is terrible in that? We were not living in all the earth even at home, nor were we together with all people. And with our friends we may be together even now, at least the true ones, who should be accorded some regard; for these would never betray or abandon us. If some are only false friends, being rid of them is better than being with them. What then? Is not the whole world the common homeland of all human beings, as Socrates thought? So, even if you leave here and go to where you were born and raised, you are not truly deprived of a homeland, only of a city, especially if one is a decent person. For such a man neither honours nor dishonours a place as a cause of happiness or unhappiness; he places everything in himself, and he considers himself a citizen of the city of Zeus, which is made up of human beings and gods. Euripides agrees with this when he says: 'For an eagle every air is passable, and for a noble man all earth is homeland.'

"Just as if a man living in his own country, or in another house than the one in which he was born, were to be distressed and lament on that account, he would be foolish and ridiculous, so too whoever lives in another city and thinks it a misfortune that he is not in the one where he happened to be born would reasonably be thought senseless and foolish. And yet regarding care of oneself and the acquisition of virtue, how could exile stand in the way? When there is neither learning nor practice that exile hinders [the exile is not hindered from either]. How would exile and its circumstances not rather provide leisure and freedom to learn noble things and to practise them more than before, since neither the fatherland, seemingly, entangles one in civic duties, nor do friends and relatives, who can hinder and pull one away from the drive toward better things? For some, indeed, exile has contributed entirely to philosophy, as it did to Diogenes, who by exile went from being a layman to a philosopher, and who, instead of sitting in Sinope, spent his time in Greece, and by his training in virtue surpassed the philosophers.

"Others, whose bodies were in poor condition from softness and luxury, grew healthy in exile, forced to live a more manly regimen. We know some who were freed from chronic diseases in exile. For example, this Spartiaticus the Lacedaemonian, who for a long time had a bad side and consequently often fell ill through luxury; when he stopped luxury, he stopped being sick. Others of the pleasure-loving, they say, were released from gout, though they had been stretched on the rack of that disease before, because exile, by accustoming them to a harder way of life, made them healthy in just that respect. So for a person in such a condition, exile helps him, body and soul, more than it hinders him.

"Nor is it the case that exiles necessarily lack the necessities. All who are idle, helpless and unable to act like men, even in their homeland are mostly in want and helpless; but the noble, the hard-working and the intelligent, wherever they come, are well supplied and spend their lives without any lack. For we need not much, if we do not wish for luxury: 'For what do mortals need except two things only, Demeter's grain and a drink of water from the vessel, which are there and grow to feed us?' I mean men worth speaking of, not those who ... ; for such men are able not only to provide easily the most necessary things for life outside their own country, but also often to earn a great deal of money. Odysseus, for instance, a fugitive as wretched as one could say, alone, naked and shipwrecked, nevertheless coming among unknown people, the Phaeacians, was able to be supplied abundantly. Themistocles, when he fled from home, came not only to friends but also to enemies and barbarians, the Persians, and received three cities as a gift, Myus, Magnesia and Lampsacus, so as to live from them. And Dion of Syracuse, stripped of his whole property by the tyrant Dionysius when he was exiled from his homeland, did so well for money in exile that he raised a foreign army, with which he came against Sicily and freed it from the tyrant. Who, then, being sensible, would look at these cases and still suppose that exile is the cause of poverty for all who are exiled?

"Nor must exiles be ill-reputed because of exile, since it is well known to all that many are justly condemned badly, many are driven out unjustly, and some good men have already been expelled by their fellow citizens: Aristides the Just at Athens, for example, and Hermodorus at Ephesus, because of whom Heraclitus said that the Ephesians should be hanged, every adult of them, since they had exiled him. Some became most famous in exile, like Diogenes of Sinope and Clearchus the Lacedaemonian, who campaigned with Cyrus against Artaxerxes; and one could name many others if one wished. How, then, could exile be a cause of bad reputation, when in exile some have become more famous than they were before?

"But, by Zeus, Euripides says exiles are deprived of freedom, since they are deprived of free speech. He has Jocasta ask Polynices what is hard for one who is exiled, and he replies: 'One thing, the greatest: he has no free speech.' And she says: 'This is a slave's lot you speak of, not to say what one thinks.' I would reply to Euripides: 'Euripides, you are right in this, that it is a slave's lot not to say what one thinks, when one must say it; for it is not always and everywhere, and not to anyone, that we must say what we think. But the other thing does not seem to me well said, that exiles lack free speech, if free speech to you means not keeping silent about what one thinks. It is not exiles who hesitate to say what they think, but those who are afraid that from saying it there will come to them toil or death or a penalty or something else of the kind. This fear, by Zeus, exile does not cause; for many people who are at home, indeed most, have fear of what seems terrible. But the brave man, though he is an exile, is no less confident toward all such things than when at home; so he speaks what he thinks no less in exile than when not in exile.' This one might say to Euripides. And you, tell me, friend: when Diogenes was an exile in Athens, or when he was sold by pirates and came to Corinth, did anyone else, Athenian or Corinthian, show more free speech than Diogenes then? Was anyone among the men of that time freer than Diogenes, who ruled even Xeniades who bought him as a master rules a slave? But why speak of old things? Do you not think I am an exile? Have I been deprived of free speech? Have I been deprived of the right to say what I think? Have you, or has anyone else, seen me cowering because I am in exile, or thinking my affairs worse now than before? By Zeus, you would not say you have seen me even grieved or discouraged because of exile. For even if someone has deprived us of our homeland, the ability to bear exile he has not taken away.

"The reasoning I use with myself so as not to be pained by exile, I might also offer to you. Exile seems to me to deprive a human being of nothing of what the many think good, as I showed just now. And even if it deprived him of some or all of these, it does not deprive him of any of the things that are truly good. For the exile is not prevented from having courage or justice, nor temperance or prudence, nor any other virtue, which, when present, naturally adorn and benefit the human being and make him praiseworthy and renowned, and, when absent, harm and shame him and make him blameworthy and without fame. This being so, if you are good and have the virtues, exile will not harm you or humble you, since the things that can most benefit and exalt you are present. But if you happen to be bad, it is vice that harms you, not exile; and it is vice that brings you grief, not exile. So hasten to be released from that rather than from exile. These things I always said to myself, and I say them now to you. And you, if you are temperate, do not consider exile terrible, which others bear easily, but vice, which makes wretched anyone who has it in him.

"For one of two things is necessary: either you are exiled justly or unjustly. If justly, how could it ever be right to be angry at the just? If unjustly, the evil belongs to those who drove you out, not to us, if indeed, by Zeus, doing injustice is most hateful to the gods, while suffering injustice, as has happened to us, is regarded by the gods and by decent human beings as deserving of aid, not of hatred."
