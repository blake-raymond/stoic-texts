# X. Whether the philosopher will bring a charge of outrage against anyone

He said he would never himself bring a charge of outrage against anyone, and would advise none of those who claim to be philosophers to do so either. "For what some people think they suffer as outrage is in none of these cases outrage or shame to those who suffer it: being reviled, being struck, being spat upon, the hardest being a blow. That such things are neither outrageous nor shameful for those who suffer them is shown by the Spartan boys, who are flogged in public and exult in that very thing. And the philosopher, if he could not despise blows and abuse, what use would he be, when he must evidently despise even death?

"'By Zeus,' someone may say, 'but the intention of the doer is terrible, when he promises and thinks to insult by slapping or abusing or doing something of the kind. Demosthenes, at any rate, holds that people commit outrage even by a look, and that such things are intolerable and drive people out of their senses.' This is how people think who are ignorant of what is truly noble and shameful and who gape at reputation, and they themselves think they are being outraged if someone looks at them too sharply or laughs at them or strikes or reviles them. But a man who has good sense and understanding, such as the philosopher should be, is disturbed by none of these, and does not think that suffering such things is shameful, but rather doing them. For what error does the one who suffers commit? The one who errs is at once also in disgrace; the one who suffers, insofar as he suffers, does not err, and is therefore in no disgrace. So the man of understanding would not go to law or bring accusations, since he would not think himself outraged. Indeed it is small-souled to be indignant or to work oneself up over such matters. He will bear what happens mildly and quietly, since this also befits one who wishes to be great-souled.

"Socrates, at any rate, was plainly so disposed. When he was reviled publicly by Aristophanes, not only was he not indignant, but meeting him he asked whether he would also like to use him for another comedy of the kind. A man who was not indignant when reviled in a theatre would be quickly angered, would he, by being reviled before a few? And Phocion the Good: when his wife was roughly handled by someone, he showed such a mild temper that, when the man in fear came to him and asked Phocion to forgive him, saying he had not known she was the wife of the man whom he was wronging, Phocion said: 'My wife has suffered nothing from you; perhaps some other woman has. So you need not make your defence to me.' And I could name many other men who, having suffered outrage, some struck in the mouth, others struck with fists so that the body felt it, are not seen defending themselves against those who struck them or pursuing them in any other way, but bearing their injustice quite mildly.

"And to consider how one may bite back the one who bit him, or repay the one who wronged him with harm, is not for a human being but for some beast, which cannot even reckon that most of those who err do so out of ignorance and lack of learning, and a person who is taught stops at once. To accept the errors of others without savagery, and not to be implacable toward those who have wronged one, but to be a cause of good hope for them, is the way of a gentle and humane man. How much better, then, that the philosopher be seen to be of this sort, deeming forgiveness due to anyone who has wronged him, than to seem to defend himself by bringing suit and accusation, and in truth to behave in a disgraceful manner, acting contrary to his own words; since he says that a good man could never be wronged by a bad one, and yet complains of being wronged by wicked people, deeming himself to be a good man."
