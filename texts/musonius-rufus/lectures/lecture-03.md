# III. That women too should do philosophy

Someone asked him whether women too should do philosophy, and he began to teach, somewhat in this way, that they should. The women, he said, have received from the gods the same reason as men, which we use toward one another and by which we consider each thing, whether it is good or bad, noble or shameful. In the same way the female has the same senses as the male: seeing, hearing, smelling, and the rest. The parts of the body are the same in each, and neither has more of them than the other. Moreover, the desire for virtue and the natural affinity for it arise not only in men but also in women; for women, no less than men, are naturally pleased by noble and just deeds, and reject the opposite. This being so, why on earth would it be fitting for men to seek and examine how they may live well, which is what philosophizing is, but not for women? Is it because men should be good, but women should not? Let us consider each of the qualities that befit a woman who is to be good. It will appear that each of them is best acquired through philosophy.

First, a woman must manage a household, calculating what benefits the home and ruling the servants. I say that these things belong most of all to the philosophizing woman, if each of them is a part of life, and philosophy is nothing other than knowledge about life, and the philosopher, as Socrates said, spends his time examining this: "whatever evil and good has come to pass in the halls" (Odyssey 4.392). The woman must also be temperate: such as to keep clean of unlawful sexual relations and of the other pleasures of licentiousness, not to be enslaved by desires, nor contentious, nor extravagant, nor fond of adornment. These are the works of the temperate woman. And besides these, she must control anger, not be overcome by grief, and be superior to every passion. The philosopher's discourse teaches these things, and the one who learns and practises them seems to me to become most well ordered, whether man or woman.

And what of justice? A woman who philosophizes would be just, a blameless partner in life, a good partner in harmony, a diligent caretaker of husband and children, entirely free of greed and the desire for more than her share. Who would be more of this kind than a philosopher, who, if she truly is a philosopher, is constrained by all necessity to consider doing injustice worse than suffering it, as it is more shameful, and to count having less as better than taking more by wrong, and moreover to love her children more than life itself? Who could be more just than a woman like that?

Further, a woman who has been educated should be braver than one who is uneducated, and the philosopher braver than the layperson, so that she would not yield to anything shameful out of fear of death or reluctance to endure toil, and would not cower before anyone because he is well born, or powerful, or rich, or by Zeus even a tyrant. For she has practised thinking big, counting death not an evil and life not a good; likewise toil not to be shunned and freedom from toil not to be pursued at all costs. Hence it is likely that such a woman would work with her own hands and endure hardship, such as to nurse her children at her own breast and serve her husband with her own hands, and do without hesitation what some people think slavish. Would not such a woman be a great benefit to the man who marries her, an ornament to her kin, and a good example to the women who know her?

But, by Zeus, some say that women who associate with philosophers must be bold and shameless, when they leave their households to live among men, practise speeches, play the sophist and analyse syllogisms, when they ought to be sitting at home spinning. I would not claim that women who philosophize, or men either, should abandon their proper tasks and devote themselves to speeches alone; rather, whatever speeches they practise should be practised for the sake of actions. Just as a medical discourse is of no use unless it leads to the health of the human body, so, if a philosopher has or teaches a discourse, it is of no use unless it leads to the virtue of the human soul. Above all, we must examine the discourse that we think philosophizing women should follow. Can the one who declares modesty the greatest good make women bold? Does the one who prescribes the greatest decorum train them to live more recklessly? Does the one who proves that licentiousness is the ultimate evil fail to teach temperance? Does the one who shows that household management is a virtue fail to urge women to manage their households? The philosopher's discourse also calls the woman to be affectionate [...] and to work with her own hands.
