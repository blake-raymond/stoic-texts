# XI. What provision of livelihood suits the philosopher

"There is another source of livelihood, none worse than this one, and perhaps, not unreasonably, thought better for a man whose body is in good condition: the land, whether one has it as his own or not. Many people who farm even another's land, public or private, can feed not only themselves but also their children and wives, and some are even very well off from this, working with their own hands and toiling hard. For the earth repays most beautifully and most justly those who care for it, giving back many times what it receives and providing an abundance of all that is necessary for life to anyone who is willing to toil, and doing so with propriety and without any shame in it. There is no human being, unless he is spoiled or soft, who would say that any of the farming works is shameful or unbecoming to a good man. How is planting not noble? How is ploughing? How is tending vines? And sowing, harvesting, threshing: are these not all free pursuits and fitting to good men? And herding, as it did not shame Hesiod or prevent him from being beloved of the gods and musical, would not prevent anyone else.

"To me this is the most agreeable of all farm work, that it allows the soul more leisure to think things through and to seek learning. For the works that strain and bend the body force the soul to be with them alone, or at least to be strained along with the body; but the works that do not too much strain the body do not prevent the soul from selecting some of the better things, and from such reflections becoming wiser itself, which every philosopher especially aims for. For this reason I particularly welcome the shepherd's life. If someone philosophizes and farms at the same time, I could compare no other life with it, nor prefer any other means of living. How could the earth, which is our nurse and mother, not be more natural for us than being fed from some other source? How is it not more manly to live in the country than to sit in a city, like the sophists? How is it not healthier to live in the open air than to be reared in the shade? 'What then? Is it not more free to provide for one's own needs than to take from others?' It is clear that not needing someone else to meet one's own needs is far more august than needing. So it is noble, happy and dear to the gods to live from farming, along with not neglecting nobility of character; hence the god proclaimed Myson of Chen wise, and called Aglaus of Psophis happy, each of whom lived in country style and used his own labour, and kept away from the pastimes of the city. Is it not worth emulating these men, imitating them, and holding to farming with zeal?

"'What then? Is it not terrible,' someone might say, 'that a man who is able to educate, and to bring the young forward into philosophy, works the land and toils like the peasants?' Yes, indeed; it would be terrible if working the land prevented philosophizing or helping others toward philosophy. But as it is, I think the young would be benefited more by associating with their teacher not in the city, hearing him speak in the school, but by seeing him working in the country and showing in deed what the argument prescribes: that one must toil and suffer in body rather than need another to feed him. What prevents the student, while working with his teacher, from hearing at the same time something about temperance, or justice, or endurance? For many arguments are not needed by those who will philosophize, and the whole crowd of theorems that we see the sophists puffed up with is not something all young people must take on; they suffice to wear out a human life. The things that are most necessary and most useful to learn are not impossible to learn even for those engaged in farm work, especially since they do not work continuously but have breaks.

"Few, indeed, will be willing to learn in this way, as I know well. But it is better for the philosopher not to take in the majority of the young who say they philosophize, the soft and the flabby, on whose account philosophy is smeared with stains when people approach it. Among true lovers of philosophy there is no one who would not be willing to spend time with a good man in the country, even if the place were extremely rough, since he would enjoy greatly the company of his teacher night and day, and keep away from the evils of the city, which stand in the way of philosophy, because one cannot escape notice there when one does something well or badly, which is the greatest benefit to those being educated. Eating, drinking and sleeping under the eye of a good man is also a great benefit. And what would come about from necessity in the association in the country, Theognis too praises, where he says: 'With them drink and eat, with them sit, and please those whose power is great.' That he says that none other than good men have great power to benefit human beings, if one is with them, drinks with them and sits with them, he shows as follows: 'From the good you will learn good things; but if you mix with the bad, you will lose even the wit you have.' So let no one say that farming is an obstacle to learning or teaching; it does not appear to be, if indeed it enables the learner to be with the teacher for the greatest length of time, and the teacher to have the learner at hand. This being so, livelihood from farming seems the most fitting for the philosopher."
