# V. Which is stronger, habit or argument?

*Lucius, from the lectures of Musonius*

The question arose again among us whether habit or argument is the more effective for acquiring virtue, supposing that argument teaches rightly what is to be done, and habit comes about by practising according to such an argument. To Musonius habit seemed more effective, and, supporting his own view, he asked one of those present, roughly like this:

"There are two doctors. One can speak well and with expert knowledge about medicine, but has no experience in treating patients. The other cannot speak, but is accustomed to treating according to the medical argument. Which of the two would you rather have at your side when you are sick?" The man answered: "The one who is accustomed to treat."

Musonius said: "And what of this? There are two men. One has sailed often and has steered many voyages to a good end; the other has sailed little and never steered. If the one who has never steered tells how one must steer most expertly, while the other is lacking in speech and altogether weak at it, which would you entrust more with steering?" "The one who has steered often," he said.

Again Musonius: "Take two musicians. One knows how to speak about music, and does so most persuasively, but cannot sing or play the cithara or lyre. The other is weaker in speech, but plays the cithara and lyre well, and sings too. To which would you entrust a musical task, or which would you wish to be the teacher of your son, who knows nothing about music?" He answered that he would choose the one who is competent in the actual performance.

"Well then," said Musonius, "that is how it stands there. But concerning temperance and self-control, is it much better to be able to say what ought to be done than to have become self-controlled and temperate in everything one does?" The young man conceded that here too, speaking about temperance well is, in practice, less and of lower worth than being temperate.

Musonius then tied this to what had been said: "How, then, in these cases would knowing the argument about each matter be better than being accustomed to do things according to the instruction of the argument? Habit leads to being able to act, whereas knowing the argument about a thing leads to being able to speak about it. Argument does cooperate with action by teaching how to act, and it comes before habit in order, since one cannot be habituated to anything noble unless one is habituated according to argument. But in power habit comes before argument, because it is more decisive in bringing a person to action than argument is."
