# XIX. On clothing and shelter

He said this about food. He also thought it right to seek a shelter [clothing and housing] that is temperate for the body, not costly and extravagant. Clothing and footwear, he said, should be used in the same way, like armour, for the protection of the body and not for display. Just as the best weapons are the strongest ones, those best able to save their user, not the showy and shining ones, so the clothing or footwear that is best is what is most useful and strongest for the body, not what can catch the eyes of fools. For the covering should make what it covers better and stronger, not weaker and worse. Those who devise smoothness and tenderness of flesh through coverings make their bodies worse, if a body that has been brought up softly is far worse than one that is hard and well exercised. But those who strengthen and toughen themselves by their covering, these alone benefit what is covered. For this reason it is not at all good to cover the body with many garments, nor to wrap the hands and feet in felts or soft fabrics, at least for those who are not ill. Nor is it good at all to make souls and bodies unacquainted with cold and heat; moderate clothing should be used, accustoming oneself to endure winter and be sunburned in summer, and to be the least shade-loving. To use one tunic is preferable to needing two, and to use none, but only a cloak, is better. To go barefoot, for one who can, is better than to wear shoes; wearing shoes is close to being bound, but going barefoot gives the feet great ease and freedom when they are trained to it. Hence one sees couriers who do not use shoes on the roads, and runners who could not maintain their speed if they had to run in shoes.

Since we also build houses for shelter, these too should be made in line with what is needed for use: to keep out cold, keep out excess heat, and be a shelter against sun and winds, adequate for those who need it. In general, what a natural cave would provide, offering a modest place of shelter for a human being, the house should provide for us, if it even exceeds that to the extent that human nourishment needs a store. What are the colonnaded courts for? What are the many-coloured stuccos? What are the gold-roofed houses? What are the costly stones, some laid low and joined together, others set into walls, some brought from very far away at the cost of huge sums? All these are superfluous and unnecessary; one can live and be healthy without them, but they require the greatest trouble and come about through much money, from which one could benefit many people, both publicly and privately. How much more glorious it is to benefit many than to live in costly style; how much more noble to spend on human beings than on wood and stone; how much more useful to possess many friends than to be surrounded by a great house, since friends are the gain of the one who eagerly does good! What would anyone get from the size and beauty of a house as much as from doing favours for the city and citizens out of his own means?
