# II. That human beings are naturally disposed toward virtue

Everyone, he said, is by nature so constituted as to live without error and nobly, not one of us and not another. A great proof of this is that lawgivers lay down for all alike what they must do and forbid what they must not, exempting no one who disobeys or does wrong, so that no one escapes punishment, neither young nor old, neither strong nor weak, no one at all. And yet, if the nature of virtue were brought in from outside and nothing of it were by nature ours, just as in the other arts no one is required to be free from error in works of an art he has not learned, so in life no one would be required to be free from error if he had not learned virtue, since virtue alone makes one not err in life. But as it is, in caring for the sick no one is expected to be free from error except the doctor; in playing the lyre, no one but the musician; in steering, no one but the helmsman. In life, however, they expect not only the philosopher, who seems alone to care for virtue, to be free from error, but all alike, even those who have made no effort at all to acquire it. Clearly, then, there is no other cause of this than that human beings are by nature inclined toward virtue.

And here is another great proof that we share in virtue by nature: all of us speak about ourselves as though we possessed virtue and were good. No ordinary person, asked whether he is foolish or prudent, will admit that he is foolish; nor, asked whether he is unjust or just, will he say that he is unjust. Likewise, if one asks whether he is temperate or intemperate, he will answer that he is temperate; and in short, if one asks whether he is good or bad, he will say good, even though he cannot name any teacher of nobility of character, nor any learning or practice of virtue he has undergone. Of what else is this a proof than that a natural foundation toward goodness exists in the human soul, and a seed of virtue in each of us? And because it is entirely fitting that we be good, some of us deceive ourselves into thinking that we already are good, while others are ashamed to admit that we are not. For why, by the gods, does no one claim to know letters, music or wrestling if he has not learned them, nor profess to have these skills without being able to name the teacher he studied under, while everyone claims to have virtue?
