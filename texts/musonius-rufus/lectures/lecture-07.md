# VII. That one should despise toil

To make those who are going to toil for the sake of virtue and nobility undergo hardship more easily and eagerly, it is useful to reckon the following. How many people toil out of evil desire, like lovers who cannot restrain themselves; how many endure for the sake of gain; how many suffer hardship in order to get a reputation. Yet all these people endure every hardship of their own free will. Is it not terrible that those people suffer these things for the sake of nothing noble, while we would not readily undergo every toil for the sake of nobility, to escape the evil that defiles our life, and to acquire virtue, which is the supplier of all good things?

And yet who could say how much better it is to toil so as to direct one's own desires than to toil to win another man's wife; to practise so as to need little, instead of labouring for money; to take trouble not to be a seeker of glory, instead of having trouble for the sake of reputation; to examine how one may envy no one, instead of seeking how to harm the person one envies; and to suffer hardship in order to gain true friends, instead of being a slave to people called friends, like flatterers. In general, since toil is necessary for all human beings, both those who aim at better things and those who aim at worse, it is quite absurd that those who pursue the better things should not be much more eager to toil than those whose hopes from their toil are small.

Consider the acrobats. They put themselves through such difficult feats and gamble with their lives: some tumble among swords, some walk high above the ground on ropes, and some, like birds, fly through the air, for whom a slip means death. And they do all this for a small wage. Shall we not endure toil for the sake of complete happiness? For the goal of becoming good is nothing other than to be happy and live blessedly for the rest of time. One could also reasonably consider what some animals do, which can easily shame us into being industrious. Quails and cocks, for instance, neither understand virtue nor know the noble and the just as a human being does, nor toil for anything of the kind; yet they fight one another and endure the beating and hold out even to death, so that neither is defeated by the other. How much more reasonable is it that we endure and persevere when we are suffering for some noble purpose: to help friends, or benefit a city, or defend wife and children; and, the greatest and most decisive of all, that we may become good, just and temperate, which comes to no one without toil. Hence, he said, they tell me that the man who is unwilling to toil almost condemns himself to be worthless for anything, because we obtain all good things by toil. These things and others like them he said then, urging and exhorting those present to despise toil.
