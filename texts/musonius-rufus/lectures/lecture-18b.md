# XVIII B. On food (continued)

"Gluttony and fancy eating are most shameful," he said, "and no one denies it. Yet as I look at how people escape them, I find that I notice very few; I see many reaching for such foods when they are absent, unable to abstain when they are present, and, when they do use them, using them without restraint, so that they use them even to the harm of the body. And what else could gluttony be but lack of restraint about food, because of which people prefer the pleasure in food to what is beneficial? Relish-eating is nothing other than immoderation in the use of relish. Immoderation is an evil everywhere, but in this it shows its nature most, presenting those who love relishes, instead of human beings, as like swine or dogs in their greed and ugliness, unable to restrain either their hands, or their eyes, or their swallowing; so the desire for the pleasure of relish drives them out of their minds.

"That this is the most shameful attitude toward food is clear, since in it we resemble senseless animals more than prudent human beings. This being so, the best thing would be the opposite: to eat in order and decorum, and to show temperance here first of all, which is not easy, but needs much care and practice [and perhaps temperance in general]. Why is this so? Because, of the many pleasures that persuade a human being to err and force him to yield to them against what is beneficial, the pleasure about food seems the hardest to fight. We meet the other pleasures more rarely, and from some we can abstain for months or whole years, but with this one we must make the attempt every day, and most often twice a day. For a human being cannot live otherwise. So the more often we try the pleasure in eating, the more numerous the dangers here. At every serving of food, in fact, there is not one danger of error, but several. The one who eats more than he should errs; the one who prepares his food for relish rather than for need errs; the one who is drawn in by relish more than he should; the one who prefers the sweeter foods to the healthier ones; the one who does not share equal portions with those who eat with him. There is another error about food too, when we offer it at the wrong time, and, putting aside something else we ought to do, eat instead. Since there are so many errors about food, the person who is going to be temperate must keep clear of all of them and be guilty of none.

"A person would be pure and free of error if he trained and habituated himself to choose food not to be pleased but to be nourished, not to soothe his throat but to strengthen his body. For the throat came to be as a passage for food, not an instrument of pleasure, and the stomach for the same reason that the root exists for every plant. Just as in plants the root feeds the plant by taking nourishment from outside, so the stomach feeds the animal from what is brought into it, grain and drink. And just as these are fed for the sake of continued existence and not for pleasure, so for us too food is a remedy for life. This is why we should eat in order to live, not in order to be pleased, if we are going to follow the best principle, that of Socrates, who said that most people live in order to eat, but he himself ate in order to live. No decent person who wishes to be a good human being will think it right to be like the many and to live for the sake of eating, like those who hunt for pleasure from food in every way.

"That the god who made the human being made him for his preservation and not for pleasure, and then furnished him food and drink, anyone could learn from this. When food is doing its work most, then, in digestion and distribution, it gives the human being no pleasure at all; we are nourished and strengthened by it then, but feel no pleasure, although this time is longer than the time in which we eat. If the god had devised food for us for the sake of pleasure, we should have been pleased by it for the longer time, not only for the shortest, in which we swallow. But nonetheless, for the sake of that shortest time, in which we feel pleasure, preparation of relishes goes on, ships sail with countless goods, and the sea is crossed to its farthest limits; cooks are busier than farmers; some serve dinners that use up whole fields, and these do the bodies no good at all from the costly foods. On the contrary, those who use the cheapest foods are the strongest. One might look, for example, at the slaves of masters, the country people compared with the townspeople, the poor compared with the rich: as a group they are sturdier and better able to work, tire less at their tasks, are sick more rarely, and put up with cold, heat, lack of sleep, and everything of the kind more easily. And yet even if costly and cheap food strengthened the body equally, the cheap would still be preferable, because it is more temperate and fits a good man better: the easily got to the hard to get, the trouble-free to what comes with trouble, the ready to the unready, more fitting for decent people.

"To say it all briefly: one should make health and strength the aim of eating, as it is for these alone that food is to be taken, and these need no luxury; and while eating we should take care for order and proper measure, and for what most distinguishes it, cleanliness and unhurried calm."
