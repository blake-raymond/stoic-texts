# XVII. What is the best provision for old age

Again, when an old man asked what would be the best provision for old age, he said: "The same as for the young: to live rightly and according to nature. You could best learn what this is if you consider that the nature of the human being was not made for pleasure. A horse was not made for pleasure, nor a dog, nor an ox, which are far less honourable than a human being. A horse would not be thought to have reached its own end if it ate and drank and mated without doing any of the things that suit a horse; nor a dog, if it enjoyed all pleasures like the horse but did nothing for which dogs are thought good; nor any other animal at all, deprived of its proper work and filled with pleasures. None of them would be said to live according to nature; rather, what lives according to nature is whatever most displays its virtue through what it does in keeping with its own nature. For the nature of each leads each toward its own virtue. So it is reasonable that the human being too does not live according to nature when he lives in pleasure, but when he lives in virtue. For then he would justly be praised, and think great of himself, and be of good hope and confident, as joy and cheerfulness necessarily follow.

"In general, the human being alone among earthly creatures is an imitation of god, and has virtues similar to his. Among the gods we can conceive of nothing better than prudence and justice, and moreover courage and temperance. So just as god, because of the presence of these virtues, is not overcome by pleasure, not overcome by greed, superior to desire, superior to envy and jealousy, high-minded, beneficent and kind to human beings (for such is how we conceive of god), so too the human being, being like him by nature, should be thought to resemble him, and, being so, should be emulated; and the one who is emulated must be happy, for we emulate none other than the happy. And such a human being is not impossible: for we have not conceived these virtues from anywhere else than from human nature itself, having met some people of this sort, whom we called godlike and divine in form.

"If, then, someone has earlier, while still young, taken care of a right education, has learned well the noble subjects that can be learned and has practised sufficiently what needs practice, such a person in old age, using the resources within himself, would live according to nature; he would bear without pain the loss of the pleasures of youth, and without pain the weakness of his body that is now present; he would not be discontented, nor despised by his neighbours, nor neglected by his own people and friends, since against all this he has a fine remedy in his own mind, the education he possesses. But if someone has come to old age lacking in education yet shows eagerness for better things and can be persuaded by those who speak well, then by hearing the advice of those who have learned what is useful he would come to know what is harmful to human beings and what beneficial, how to avoid some and acquire the others, and how to receive gently the things that are not bad but seem so. By hearing these and obeying (for hearing without obeying is altogether unprofitable) he would arrange his old age well, and everything else, and he would above all rid himself of the fear of death, which most disturbs and oppresses the old, as though they had forgotten that death is owed by every mortal. The most wretched thing that makes life miserable for old people is just this, the fear of death; as the orator Isocrates plainly admitted. They say that when someone asked him how he was getting on, he said: 'As is reasonable for a man who has reached ninety years and considers death the last of evils.' And yet how would he have any share of education or of knowledge of what is truly good or bad, who supposed that what follows by necessity on the best life is evil? For if the life of the good man is the best, its end is death. What I said, then, if one gains this in old age, to accept death fearlessly and confidently, that would contribute much to living painlessly and according to nature. One may gain it by associating with those who are philosophers not in name but in truth, if one is willing to be persuaded.

"I say, then, that the best provision for old age is what I said when I began the discussion: to live according to nature, doing and thinking what one should. For in this way the old man would be most cheerful and most praiseworthy, and with these would live easily and honourably. If someone thinks that wealth is the greatest consolation for the old, and that it provides a painless life for them, he is wrong. Wealth can supply people with the pleasures that come from food, drink, sex and the like, but cheerfulness and freedom from pain it can in no way give to its owner. There are many witnesses to this among the rich, who are grieving and despondent and call themselves wretched. So wealth would not even be a good aid for old age."
