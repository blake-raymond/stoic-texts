# XV A. Whether every child that is born should be raised

The lawgivers, whose very task it is to seek out and consider what is good for a city and what bad, what benefits the commonwealth and what harms it, all held that the increase of the citizens' households is most profitable for cities, and their decrease most harmful. They took childlessness, or having few children, to be unprofitable, and having children, even many, by Zeus, to be profitable. So they forbade women to procure abortion, and laid down a penalty for women who disobeyed; they added penalties for childlessness, and forbade women to prevent conception; and they set a prize of honour for those with many children, men and women, and made childlessness a penalty. How, then, would we not be acting unjustly and unlawfully, by doing the opposite of what the lawgivers wished, divinely inspired and god-beloved men, whom it is thought noble and advantageous to follow? We see that we act contrary to them by hindering our own large family. How would we not also sin against our ancestral gods and against Zeus of the kindred by doing this? Just as one who is unjust to guests sins against Zeus the protector of guests, and one who is unjust to friends against Zeus of friendship, so whoever is unjust to his own kin sins against the ancestral gods and against Zeus of the kindred, who oversees offences concerning families; and the one who sins against the gods is impious.

That rearing children is noble and profitable one could learn by reckoning how a man with many children is honoured in the city, how he gives reverence to his neighbours, and how he can do more than all his equals, provided his children are not unlike themselves in prospering. Just as a man with many friends is more powerful than one without friends, so also much more is a man with many children than one who is childless or has few; and by as much as every son is closer than a friend. It is worth considering what a sight it is: a man or woman with many children, seen all together with their own children. Neither a procession sent to the gods would anyone see so beautiful, nor a chorus dancing at sacred rites so worthy of the sight, as a chorus of many children marching ahead of their father or mother in the city, leading their parents by the hand or looking after them in some other way. What is more beautiful than this sight? What is more enviable than such parents, especially if they are decent? For whom else would one pray so readily to the gods for good things, or work with so willingly toward whatever they need? "By Zeus," someone says, "but ..." [the text breaks off].
